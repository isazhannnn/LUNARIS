## Summary

LUNARIS - The Last Signal is an educational browser game by Parsec Studio, the team of Meiirzhan Issa and Zhasulan Zholdas. We want school students and anyone curious about space to explore lunar science without complicated setup or specialist knowledge. Players simply open the website and start: no download or installation is required. Our original concept follows Alex, an engineer at a damaged lunar outpost. Players recover water, build protection, restore communication and explore for ice. Four controllable missions connect practical actions with short explanations and questions. PIX offers hints when something is unclear. Selected conditions vary between runs, and the game supports English, Russian, keyboard and touch controls. NASA resources inform the scientific explanations. In informal playtesting, friends and acquaintances completed all four missions and Alex's story without help from our team, using PIX's hints when needed.

## Detailed project description

We are Parsec Studio: Meiirzhan Issa and Zhasulan Zholdas. We created LUNARIS - The Last Signal to make learning about the Moon approachable for school students and anyone curious about space. Players can open the website and start without downloading software or having specialist scientific knowledge. Our original concept combines the story of an engineer left behind after an evacuation with practical science missions. The project is our response to Build a Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer.

The player controls Alex, who remains at a damaged station with a helper robot called PIX. Power, air, water, food, protection and communication all need attention, but resources are limited. The player has to decide what to repair and how to use the available equipment. The story gives each task a reason: help Alex restore the outpost and reconnect with Earth.

Four science expeditions develop this idea through different activities. In Water Recovery, players carry water between treatment stations, follow the correct process, adjust valves and test the result before storage. In Storm Shelter, they compare two materials against mass and protection limits, then position protective panels. In Earth Link, they calculate the time needed for a signal to travel to Earth and back, calibrate the equipment and wait for a reply. In Ice Crater, they prepare and drive a rover, collect an ice sample without overheating the drill, and keep enough energy for the return journey.

Players first try a task and see what happens. They then receive an explanation and answer a short question about the principle behind it. PIX provides hints along the way. Selected layouts, resource limits, communication distances, sample positions and questions vary between runs. Progress and badges are saved in the browser.

In informal playtesting, our friends and acquaintances completed all four science missions and Alex's story without help from our team. When they were unsure how to proceed, they used PIX's in-game hints.

We used nine NASA resources to inform the educational explanations, including material about life support, plants in space, radiation protection, signal travel time, lunar ice and a historical space-weather event. The repository explains where each resource is used. Our numerical models are simplified for learning; the game does not use live NASA telemetry.

LUNARIS runs in a browser on computers and phones, with keyboard and touch controls and English and Russian interfaces. An offline HTML edition is also included. The implementation uses HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Canvas 2D, Web Audio and local storage. Automated checks cover mission completion, resource constraints, signal timing and generated scenarios. The source code and checks are public.

The original project concept and creative direction belong to our team. We used ChatGPT to assist with selected coding, debugging, testing, research, writing and translation tasks. Image-generation tools helped produce fictional backgrounds and presentation artwork, which are visibly identified. The team selected the final materials and directed their integration.

We believe LUNARIS deserves recognition because its scientific ideas have consequences the player can see and test. Completing a task means planning, handling equipment and explaining a decision. We have delivered a working game that judges can play and examine. Its educational benefit is our goal; classroom learning outcomes have not yet been formally measured.
